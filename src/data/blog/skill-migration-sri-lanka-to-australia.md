A quick bit of context - I'm a software engineer who migrated from Sri Lanka to Australia through the skill migration pathway. I've written about [the decisions that led me to make the move](/posts/the-decisions-that-led-me-to-australia) in a separate post if you're curious about the backstory.

Since moving, I've had quite a few people reach out to me asking how the process works. So I thought it might be useful to put together a high-level guide for anyone who is in a similar situation and wondering where to start.

**Important: This is not immigration or legal advice. This is simply a summary of my personal experience and general information that is publicly available. Immigration rules change frequently, so always verify everything with official sources or a registered migration agent (MARA-registered) before making any decisions.**

## Agent or DIY?

The first decision you'll need to make is whether you want to use a migration agent or do everything yourself. I personally used an agent, and it made the process smoother because they guided me through each step. But I also have friends who handled the entire thing on their own and made it to Australia just fine.

If you're going the agent route, do your research. Google reviews, word of mouth from friends who have already migrated, and online forums are all good starting points. I'm not going to name any specific agents here, but there are plenty of reputable ones in Sri Lanka if you look around.

If you're going the DIY route, be prepared to spend a lot of time reading through the Department of Home Affairs website and understanding the requirements yourself.

## Step 1: Check if you meet the criteria

Before anything else, you need to check whether you're eligible. Australia's skill migration is a points-based system. Your points are calculated based on a combination of factors like your age, work experience, education level, and English language proficiency.

<!-- [ADDED BY CLAUDE] I added the minimum 65 points detail below since this is a key threshold that readers would want to know. -->

You need a minimum of 65 points to be eligible, but realistically, the higher your score the better your chances. For competitive occupations like software engineering, invitation scores have been significantly higher than 65 in recent rounds.

If you're applying with a spouse, their qualifications and English scores can also add to your total points.

## Step 2: Get your documents in order

This is the part that takes more effort than you'd expect. You'll need to gather documentation from your previous workplaces - service letters, payslips, and other employment evidence. And here's the thing - these documents need to be in a specific format. In my case, some of my older service letters didn't meet the format required by Australian immigration, so I had to go back to previous employers and get them redone.

Make sure every service letter is on company letterhead, signed by an authorised person, and clearly states your role, duties, and employment dates. Don't leave this to the last minute because chasing down old employers is not fun.

## Step 3: Skills assessment

If you're a software engineer or any other IT professional, your skills need to be assessed by the Australian Computer Society (ACS). This is a mandatory step before you can apply for a visa.

ACS will look at your qualifications and work experience to determine if they meet Australian standards. You'll generally need a bachelor's degree relevant to your profession, though ACS does have alternative pathways such as Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL) for those with significant work experience but different qualifications.

<!-- [ADDED BY CLAUDE] I added the RPL mention above since ACS offers this pathway and it's useful for readers who may not have a traditional ICT degree. -->

A few things to keep in mind with the ACS assessment - they assess your actual job duties, not your job title. So make sure your reference letters describe what you actually did in detail. Also, ACS typically deducts a portion of your work experience (usually 2-4 years depending on your qualification) to account for the time needed to reach professional competency. Only the experience after this deduction counts toward your migration points.

## Step 4: English language test

You'll need to take either IELTS or PTE Academic to prove your English proficiency. I'd personally suggest PTE if you're a software engineer or someone who is comfortable with computers. The test is entirely computer-based, and from what I've seen, people in tech tend to find it more natural than the paper-based components of IELTS.

The higher you score, the more points you get. So it's worth spending some time preparing for this. If your spouse is also applying, their English score can contribute additional points too.

## Step 5: Consider NAATI CCL (optional)

This is something I didn't end up doing, but it's worth mentioning. The NAATI Credentialed Community Language (CCL) test can earn you an extra 5 points. It tests your ability to interpret everyday conversations between English and another language. If you're bilingual and your points are borderline, this could make the difference.

## Step 6: Medical examinations

You, your spouse, and any dependents will need to complete medical examinations from a panel-approved doctor. One thing to note - medical results have an expiry, typically around 12 months. If your application takes longer than expected (which it very well might), you may have to redo them. I had to go through this more than once, so be prepared for that possibility.

The good news is that your other documents like service letters and English test results generally don't expire in the same way, so you won't have to redo everything.

## Step 7: State nomination (ROI)

Once everything above is sorted, the next step is to submit an Expression of Interest (EOI) through SkillSelect and then a Registration of Interest (ROI) for state nomination.

You can apply for nomination from specific states - for example, if you only want to live in New South Wales or Victoria. But my suggestion would be to keep your options open and apply for as many states as possible. It increases your chances of getting nominated.

Each state has its own requirements and point thresholds, so what works for one state might not work for another.

## Step 8: Wait, then apply for the visa

Once you get nominated (fingers crossed), you can then apply for the actual visa. This involves paying the visa application fee and submitting your formal application to the Department of Home Affairs.

Then you wait again. And sometimes during this waiting period, your medical examination might expire, which means you'll need to do it again.

## Step 9: The outcome

If everything is in order, you'll eventually receive your PR grant. In my experience, once you get the state nomination, you're in a strong position. But it's not 100% guaranteed - applications can still be refused if there are inconsistencies in your documentation or if incorrect information was provided earlier. So make sure everything is accurate from the very beginning. Going back and fixing things at this stage is not something you want to deal with after waiting for over a year.

The whole process can take anywhere from one to three years, sometimes even longer depending on factors outside your control. Be patient and be thorough.
